Key Discovery

⚗ Complete Theory & Explanation

Before the Experiment — Thomson's Plum Pudding Model▾

In 1904, J.J. Thomson proposed the Plum Pudding Model. He imagined the atom as a diffuse, uniform sphere of positive charge (the "pudding") with tiny electrons embedded throughout (the "plums").

In this model, positive charge is spread evenly — so no region is dense enough to cause large deflections. If Thomson's model were correct, all alpha particles would pass through with only tiny deflections of less than a few degrees.

Rutherford initially expected exactly this result — that the particles would sail straight through like cannonballs through tissue paper.

The Experimental Setup (1909)▾
  • Alpha Source: Radioactive Radium-226 emits alpha particles — helium nuclei (2 protons + 2 neutrons), charge +2.
  • Collimator: A lead block with a narrow slit focuses alpha particles into a precise beam.
  • Gold Foil Target: Extremely thin gold foil (~0.4 micrometres — about 1000 atoms thick). Gold was chosen for its extreme ductility — it can be beaten impossibly thin.
  • ZnS Detector Screen: Zinc Sulfide screen surrounding the foil — flashes (scintillates) when struck by an alpha particle.
  • Geiger & Marsden (Rutherford's assistants) patiently counted flashes at all angles — including directly behind the foil.
Observations & What They Proved▾
  • ~98% of particles: Passed straight through → The atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some particles: Deflected at small angles → Slight electrostatic (Coulomb) repulsion from the nucleus.
  • ~1 in 8000 particles: Bounced back at angles greater than 90° → An extremely small, dense, positively charged region at the center (the nucleus).

Rutherford famously said: "It was almost as incredible as if you fired 15-inch shells at tissue paper and they came back and hit you."

The back-scattering proved that the positive charge and most of the mass must be concentrated in an incredibly tiny central region — the nucleus.

Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911)▾

Based on his results, Rutherford proposed a revolutionary new atomic model:

  • The atom has a tiny, extremely dense, positively charged nucleus at its center.
  • The nucleus contains almost all of the atom's mass but occupies an incredibly small volume.
  • Electrons orbit the nucleus at a relatively vast distance — like planets around the Sun.
  • The atom is mostly empty space — the nucleus is ~100,000 times smaller than the atom itself.
  • The positive nuclear charge deflects positively charged alpha particles via Coulomb repulsion (like charges repel).
  • The closer an alpha particle passes to the nucleus, the stronger the repulsion and the greater the deflection angle.
The Physics — Coulomb's Law & Scattering▾

The deflection of alpha particles is governed by Coulomb's Law:

F = k · q₁q₂ / r²

  • F = electrostatic repulsion force
  • k = Coulomb's constant (8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²)
  • q₁, q₂ = charges of alpha particle (+2e) and gold nucleus (+79e)
  • r = distance between them

When an alpha particle passes far from the nucleus, r is large, force is small → small deflection. When it passes very close, r is tiny, force is enormous → large deflection or backscatter. Only 1 in 8000 particles gets close enough to the nucleus to bounce back.

Scientific Significance & Legacy▾

The Gold Foil Experiment is one of the most important experiments in the history of science:

  • Established the nuclear model of the atom — foundation of modern atomic physics.
  • Completely disproved Thomson's Plum Pudding Model.
  • Led Rutherford to discover the proton in 1917.
  • Inspired Niels Bohr to develop the Bohr Model (1913) with quantized electron orbits.
  • Laid groundwork for nuclear physics, nuclear energy, and nuclear medicine.
  • The concept of Rutherford scattering is still used today in Rutherford Backscattering Spectrometry (RBS) to analyze thin film materials.
  • Gold was used because of its extreme ductility — it can be hammered into foils just a few hundred atoms thick without breaking.
