Key Discovery

Rutherford's experiment shattered the Thomson Plum Pudding Model. Most alpha particles passed straight through — proving atoms are mostly empty space. The rare large-angle deflections revealed a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the center, surrounded by electrons at a relatively vast distance.

Before the Experiment — Thomson's Model ▾

In 1904, J.J. Thomson proposed the Plum Pudding Model. He imagined the atom as a diffuse sphere of positive charge (the "pudding") with electrons embedded throughout (the "plums"). In this model, the positive charge is spread uniformly — so no region is dense enough to cause large deflections.

If Thomson's model were correct, all alpha particles would pass through with only tiny deflections of less than a few degrees. Rutherford initially expected exactly this result.

The Experimental Setup ▾
  • Source: Radioactive radium emits alpha particles (helium nuclei — 2 protons + 2 neutrons).
  • Collimator: A lead block with a narrow slit focuses alpha particles into a beam.
  • Target: An extremely thin gold foil (~0.4 micrometres — about 1000 atoms thick).
  • Detector: A movable zinc sulfide (ZnS) screen that produces a flash of light when struck by an alpha particle.
  • Geiger & Marsden counted flashes at various angles around the foil, including behind the source.
Observations & What They Proved ▾
  • Most particles (~98%): Passed straight through or with very small deflections → The atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some particles: Were deflected at small angles → Slight electrostatic repulsion exists.
  • ~1 in 8000 particles: Bounced back at angles > 90° → An extremely small, dense, positively charged region exists at the center.

Rutherford famously said: "It was almost as incredible as if you fired 15-inch shells at tissue paper and they came back and hit you."

Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911) ▾

Based on the results, Rutherford proposed a revolutionary new model:

  • The atom has a tiny, extremely dense, positively charged nucleus at its center.
  • The nucleus contains almost all of the atom's mass.
  • Electrons orbit the nucleus at a relatively vast distance, like planets around the sun.
  • The atom is mostly empty space — the nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom.
  • The positive charge of the nucleus is what deflects positively charged alpha particles via Coulomb repulsion.
Scientific Significance & Legacy ▾

The Gold Foil Experiment is considered one of the most important experiments in the history of science.

  • It established the nuclear model of the atom, which is the foundation of modern atomic physics.
  • It disproved Thomson's Plum Pudding Model completely.
  • It led to the discovery of the proton (Rutherford, 1917).
  • It inspired Niels Bohr to develop the Bohr model (1913) with quantized electron orbits.
  • It laid the groundwork for nuclear physics and ultimately nuclear energy and medicine.
  • Gold was used because it can be beaten into extremely thin sheets without breaking (high ductility).
