Bohr's Postulates

Electrons revolve in fixed circular orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy. Each orbit has a quantized angular momentum: mvr = nh/2π. Energy is only emitted or absorbed when an electron jumps between shells, releasing a photon of energy E = hν = E₂ − E₁.

⚗ Complete Theory & Explanation

Before Bohr — Rutherford's Limitation (1911)▾

Rutherford's nuclear model (1911) proved the atom had a dense nucleus at its center with electrons orbiting far away. However, it had a fatal flaw: according to classical electrodynamics, an electron orbiting a nucleus would continuously radiate energy (as electromagnetic waves), spiral inward, and crash into the nucleus within ~10⁻¹⁰ seconds.

Rutherford's model also could not explain the discrete line spectrum of hydrogen — why hydrogen emitted only specific colors of light (e.g., the Balmer series in the visible range) and not a continuous spectrum.

Niels Bohr solved both problems in 1913 by introducing quantum conditions into the atomic model.

Bohr's Three Postulates (1913)▾
  • Postulate 1 — Stationary Orbits: Electrons revolve around the nucleus in certain fixed circular orbits called stationary states or allowed orbits. While in these orbits, electrons do NOT emit radiation — they have constant energy.
  • Postulate 2 — Quantized Angular Momentum: Only those orbits are permitted where the angular momentum (L) of the electron is an integral multiple of h/2π:
    mvr = nh / 2π    (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)
    Here m = electron mass, v = speed, r = orbit radius, h = Planck's constant, n = principal quantum number (shell number).
  • Postulate 3 — Quantum Jumps & Photon Emission: When an electron jumps from a higher energy orbit (n₂) to a lower energy orbit (n₁), it emits a photon of energy:
    E = hν = E₂ − E₁
    When it absorbs a photon of the exact same energy, it jumps upward (excitation).
Derivation — Bohr's Radius & Energy Formula▾

Combining Coulomb's attraction with centripetal force and the quantization condition, Bohr derived:

rₙ = n² × a₀    where a₀ = 0.529 Å (Bohr Radius)
  • n=1: r₁ = 0.529 Å — ground state, smallest orbit
  • n=2: r₂ = 2.116 Å — 4× larger than ground state
  • n=3: r₃ = 4.761 Å — 9× larger

The energy of each orbit is:

Eₙ = −13.6 / n²   eV
  • Negative sign means the electron is bound to the nucleus.
  • Higher n → less negative energy → electron is farther from nucleus and loosely bound.
  • n = ∞ means the electron is ionized (free); ionisation energy = 13.6 eV for hydrogen.
Spectral Lines — Hydrogen Emission Series▾

When electrons fall from higher shells to specific lower shells, they emit photons whose wavelengths form distinct spectral series named after their discoverers:

  • Lyman Series (n→1): Ultraviolet region. Most energetic transitions.
  • Balmer Series (n→2): Visible light region. The famous red (Hα, 656 nm), cyan (Hβ, 486 nm) lines.
  • Paschen Series (n→3): Infrared region.
  • Brackett Series (n→4): Far infrared.
  • Pfund Series (n→5): Far infrared.

The wavelength formula (Rydberg equation):

1/λ = R∞ (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)
R∞ = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (Rydberg constant)

Successes of the Bohr Model▾
  • Accurately predicted the line spectrum of hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions (He⁺, Li²⁺).
  • Calculated the ionisation energy of hydrogen as 13.6 eV — in perfect agreement with experiment.
  • Explained why atoms are stable (electrons don't spiral in because they occupy quantized orbits).
  • Introduced the concept of principal quantum number n, which became fundamental to quantum mechanics.
  • Explained the origin of spectral lines: each line corresponds to an electron transition between two specific shells.
  • Successfully reproduced the experimentally known Rydberg constant from first principles.
Limitations of the Bohr Model▾
  • Could not explain the spectra of multi-electron atoms (helium, lithium, etc.).
  • Could not explain the fine structure (splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields — Zeeman effect).
  • Assumed circular orbits, but electrons actually exist in probability clouds (orbitals), not fixed paths.
  • Violated the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle — you cannot simultaneously know an electron's exact position and momentum.
  • Could not explain chemical bonding or the intensities of spectral lines.
  • Eventually replaced by the Quantum Mechanical Model (Schrödinger, 1926) which uses wave functions (ψ) to describe electrons.
Bohr vs Rutherford — Quick Comparison▾
  • Rutherford (1911): Nucleus exists; electrons orbit freely like planets. No quantization. Unstable (predicts electron spiraling).
  • Bohr (1913): Added quantum rules — only specific orbits allowed; electrons stable in orbits; energy emitted/absorbed only during jumps.
  • Key addition: Bohr used Planck's quantum theory (E=hν) and applied it to atomic structure for the first time.
  • Legacy: Bohr's model was a bridge between classical physics and modern quantum mechanics, earning him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922.
